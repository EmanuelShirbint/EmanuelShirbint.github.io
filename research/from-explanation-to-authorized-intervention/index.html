Primary-source research explainer · 7 August 2026

Evidence can support an explanation. It cannot grant authority to act.

A new public preprint by Emanuel Shirbint proposes a governed closed loop for medical digital twins in multiple-sclerosis care. Its central rule is deliberately narrow: an explanation, however plausible or well supported, must not move directly into clinical intervention.

Preprint · Version 1 Not peer reviewed Conceptual architecture No clinical deployment claim Not medical advice No autonomous treatment authority
Claim boundary. The primary record describes a conceptual architecture and proposed validation path. It does not report a deployed medical device, a clinical trial, autonomous prescribing, validated patient-outcome improvement, regulatory approval, or permission to bypass qualified clinical judgment and patient consent.

The paper separates two questions that fluent AI systems can easily blur: What is probably happening? and What should be done, by whom, under whose authority? The first is epistemic. The second is clinical, institutional, legal, and personal.

The governed path

The proposed loop keeps each transition visible so that a recommendation cannot acquire borrowed authority merely because it appears inside a sophisticated system.

1. Evidence
Typed, time-stamped observations with provenance.
2. Patient state
A temporal representation that preserves unknowns and contradictions.
3. Hypotheses
Competing explanations, counter-evidence, and an explicit unknown state.
4. Causal review
Separation of association, mechanism, and intervention effect.
5. Governance gates
Eligibility, evidence quality, scope, consent, and escalation checks.
6. Authorized decision
A legitimate human decision state, not an approval ornament.
7. Action contract
Owner, timing, monitoring, stop rules, and accountability.
8. Outcomes
Observed consequences return as new evidence without overwriting history.

What the gates are designed to prevent

GateQuestion it preservesUnsafe shortcut it blocks
Evidence integrityAre source, identity, measurement, and transformation records trustworthy?Treating generated or mislinked data as observed fact.
Temporal validityIs the evidence recent enough for this decision?Turning stale records into current clinical certainty.
Competing hypothesesWhat contradicts the leading explanation, and what remains unknown?Premature closure around a fluent narrative.
Causal supportDoes the evidence support an intervention effect, not only an association?Presenting correlation as a treatment rationale.
EligibilityDo indication, risk, contraindications, alternatives, and monitoring support consideration?Confusing a plausible option with a suitable action.
Authority and consentWho may decide, and has the patient authorized the relevant use?Borrowing legitimacy from a model, institution, or interface.
Safe learningCan outcomes update the system without erasing the original recommendation and decision?Rewriting history or learning silently from ungoverned action.

Human oversight must be observable

The preprint proposes an oversight-quality profile rather than treating a clicked approval button as proof of meaningful review. Relevant signals include whether evidence was inspected, whether alternatives were considered, how long the review took, whether reasoned dissent occurred, and whether escalation remained possible. A low override rate alone cannot distinguish an accurate system from ceremonial approval.

Urgency changes the route, not the invariant

The architecture allows a bounded emergency route through pre-authorized delegation and mandatory retrospective ratification. That is not an exemption from authorization. It changes who authorizes and when the authorization is reviewed while keeping an authorized decision state on the path to action.
