Generative artificial intelligence has sharply reduced the technical cost of producing scientific text. Increased production capacity does not, however, entail cumulative knowledge, conceptual novelty, or epistemic progress. This paper introduces the Reinforcement Learning Contour (RLC) as a conceptual and architectural framework for shifting AI-assisted research from repetitive content generation toward governed recursive learning. The RLC is not proposed as a conventional reinforcement-learning algorithm and does not claim equivalence with a Markov decision process, scalar reward function, value function, or policy-optimization procedure. The framework distinguishes two analytical levels: an epistemic contour is the time-indexed state of a bounded configuration of knowledge, ontology, relational structure, retrieval mechanisms, evaluative criteria, inquiry policy, provenance, memory, tools, and governance constraints; the Reinforcement Learning Contour is the governed recursive architecture that links such states through evidence, action, evaluation, approval, and reintegration. The central proposition is a differentiation principle: the value of a research cycle is proportional not to the volume of output it generates, but to the beneficial and traceable differentiation it produces between the preceding epistemic contour and the contours governing subsequent perception, interpretation, decision, and action. This reframes the unit of scientific analysis from the document to the transformation of the mechanism that produced the document. The paper positions the principle relative to Lakatos's progressive and degenerating problem shifts, Popperian criticism, and double-loop learning; specifies a role-based architecture in which provenance, temporality, and human acceptance authority are structural rather than procedural; provides a Structured Epistemic Change Record that documents differentiation without reducing scientific quality to a single optimized score; enumerates failure modes and controls; and states seven falsifiable hypotheses with a comparative evaluation design. One local implementation is described as an existence proof, combining a prior-research corpus, a governed knowledge store, a temporal provenance graph, source-grounded synthesis, adversarial external discovery, and local orchestration under sole human acceptance authority. The framework is explicitly presented as an untested conceptual proposal. A research cycle may be epistemically successful even when it produces no publishable document, provided it improves the mechanism from which future inquiry emerges.

Keywords — reinforcement learning contour, AI-assisted scientific inquiry, epistemic transformation, research programmes, double-loop learning, provenance, temporal knowledge graphs, agentic science, human governance, scientific methodology

1.Research problem

Generative AI has sharply reduced the technical cost of producing scientific text, but increased production capacity does not by itself entail cumulative knowledge, conceptual novelty, or epistemic progress. The paper asks what unit of analysis and what architecture would let AI-assisted inquiry advance the mechanism of inquiry rather than merely multiply documents.

2.Contribution

  • Introduces the Reinforcement Learning Contour (RLC): a governed recursive architecture that links time-indexed epistemic contours through evidence, action, evaluation, approval and reintegration — explicitly not a conventional reinforcement-learning algorithm, reward function or policy-optimization procedure.
  • States a differentiation principle: the value of a research cycle is proportional to the beneficial, traceable differentiation it produces between epistemic contours, not to the volume of output generated — reframing the unit of scientific analysis from the document to the transformation of the mechanism that produced it.
  • Positions the principle relative to Lakatos's progressive and degenerating problem shifts, Popperian criticism and double-loop learning; specifies a role-based architecture in which provenance, temporality and human acceptance authority are structural rather than procedural; provides a Structured Epistemic Change Record; enumerates failure modes and controls; and states seven falsifiable hypotheses with a comparative evaluation design.
