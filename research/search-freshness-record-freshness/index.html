A fresh search result is not a fresh research claim

Search crawl time, publication time, registry time, and citation-observation time answer different questions. Keeping those clocks separate prevents a newly visible page from becoming a falsely new achievement.

Four clocks in one public result

ClockWhat it can establishWhat it cannot establish
Search crawlA search engine recently discovered or refreshed a page.That the underlying work is new, peer-reviewed, cited, current, or authoritative.
Publisher postingThe date and version that a publisher made public, together with the publisher's status label.That a later crawl date created a later version or changed review status.
Registry deposit or indexingWhen metadata entered Crossref, Europe PMC, PubMed, or another bounded index.That index arrival equals publication, a unique work, or a citation event.
Citation observationA count observed in a named source on a named date under that source's coverage rules.A source-independent impact total or a stable fact that can be copied across platforms.

A safe update rule

  1. Use search results to discover a candidate source, not to settle the claim.
  2. Resolve the candidate to a publisher, DOI registry, or bounded bibliographic index.
  3. Compare title, authors, persistent identifier, version, date, and publication-status label.
  4. Classify the event as search refresh, index propagation, new version, or new work.
  5. Change the public catalogue only when the evidence class itself changed.

Claim boundaries

Peer-reviewed work, conference work, preprints, conceptual research, the bounded empirical preprint, software, the locally evidenced EWCC production system, employment, and advisory work remain separate evidence classes. A recent crawl cannot transfer evidence between those classes. This guide makes no claim of clinical deployment, regulatory approval, external production validation, present employment, advisory engagement, institutional representation, or citation impact.
