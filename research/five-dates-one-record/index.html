Publication evidence · date provenance

Five dates, one research record

Submitted, posted, electronically published, assigned to an issue, and indexed are different events. A reliable citation names the event it dates.

Status: Primary-source date guide · Current public-record snapshot · No new canonical work · No new DOI version · No peer-review inference from registry timing · No citation-count claim · No employment or advisory claim

Why one work can carry several correct dates

A publisher records events in the work's own lifecycle. A bibliographic registry records events in the record's lifecycle. Those clocks answer different questions. A submitted date can document manuscript provenance; a posted or electronic-publication date can document public availability; a print or issue date can document journal assignment; a registry-created or indexed timestamp can document metadata processing. None of those fields, by itself, changes a preprint into a peer-reviewed article.

A citation-safe date protocol

  1. Identify the manifestation: journal article, conference contribution, preprint version, software release, or another record type.
  2. Prefer the version-specific identifier: retain a Version 2 DOI as Version 2 and preserve Version 1 only as lineage provenance.
  3. Name the date event: submitted, posted, electronic, print, issue, collection, registry-created, or registry-indexed.
  4. Keep status independent: peer-reviewed work, conference work, preprints, conceptual research, and bounded empirical research require explicit labels regardless of date.
  5. Treat late registry activity as discovery evidence: verify the publisher record before asserting a new work, version, or status transition.
